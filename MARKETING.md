Alex Hormozi would turn it into an offer and score it on his value equation: dream outcome, likelihood, time and effort.

Dream outcome: "My most important photos outlive my phone, my accounts and every app."
Likelihood: you can see which friends hold your photos, and the app shows it.
Time and effort: three taps, then a scheduled moment.
He'd name the enemy (platforms that own your memories) and stack the bonuses: the verification code, streaks, circle stats, and the belly backup. A line from him might be "Your photos, kept alive by the people who love you, for free."

Katelyn Bourgoin would start from the moment of struggle, using the customer's own words. She'd interview people who recently lost photos or left a platform and ask what pushed them, what pulled them, what they tried instead and what held them back.

Pushes: a dead phone, a hacked account, a feed that makes every share feel like a performance.
Pulls: a ritual with one person, and nothing to manage.
Anxieties: "will my friend actually keep this?" and "what if I lose the key?"
Her copy would quote those people directly and lead with the struggle ("I keep paying to store photos nobody sees") before any feature.

Marty Neumeier would give you a sentence to fill in and a gut feeling to aim at. His "onlyness" statement: Our app is the only photo-sharing app that keeps your photos alive in the browsers of the people you love, for people who want their memories to outlast any platform. He'd flip the framing from "what we built" to "who you become by using it," a keeper of a circle, and look for the zag: everyone else sells storage, you sell kinship.

The belly idea

There's a lot less to build than it sounds. Every rendezvous already does it: the photo you send your brother lives in his browser. A first version needs no new mechanism, only three things:

Ask the browser for persistent storage, since browsers can evict site data under pressure.
Add a "restore from friends" action that asks each person to send back the photos you gave them.
Show a gentle "held by 3 friends" indicator on each photo.

Later, you could add deliberate replication. Encrypt each photo, and split it into pieces so that any 3 of 5 friends can reconstruct it. Friends hold pieces they can't read, which also protects them from storing something they never saw.

What I'd say honestly: browsers are poor archives. Storage can be cleared, devices die, and friends drift away. The promise should be "some survive," exactly as you put it, never "safe forever." The hardest part is the key, because if you lose it, the encrypted pieces are useless. A recovery phrase or shares of the key held by trusted friends solves it, but it's a real design job.
